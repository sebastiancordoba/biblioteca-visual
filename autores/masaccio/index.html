Pintor florentino

Masaccio

1401–c. 1428

Masaccio nació el 21 de diciembre de 1401 en Castel San Giovanni, hoy San Giovanni Valdarno 1. En enero de 1422 ya figuraba como «pictor» en el gremio florentino de Medici e Speziali 1. En 1426 pintó el políptico del Carmine de Pisa, cuya tabla central está en la National Gallery 12. Trabajó «codo con codo» con Masolino en la capilla Brancacci, y tuvo a Brunelleschi por maestro de perspectiva en la Trinidad de Santa Maria Novella 1. Sus figuras pesan y reciben la luz de una sola fuente 4. Murió en Roma hacia 1428, antes del 18 de noviembre de 1429 15. Vasari enumera a quienes estudiaron la Brancacci, de Leonardo a Miguel Ángel 3.

Muerte: ULAN y la Enciclopedia Treccani dan 1428 [4][5]; el DBI solo fija que murió en Roma antes del 18 de noviembre de 1429, según una nota del catastro florentino, y lo documenta vivo en Florencia el 29 de julio de 1427 [1].

Lugar de nacimiento: ULAN enlaza «Castel San Giovanni» con el homónimo de la provincia de Piacenza (Emilia-Romaña), aunque su propia nota lo sitúa entre Florencia y Arezzo [4]; el DBI lo identifica con San Giovanni Valdarno, provincia de Arezzo [1]. El registro geográfico de ULAN es erróneo.

Edad: la National Gallery dice que murió a los 27 [2]; con nacimiento en diciembre de 1401 y muerte en 1428 tendría 26. La traducción de Vasari en Wikisource arrastra en nota fechas decimonónicas desechadas (1402–1443) [3].

Nota biográfica del Getty

Masaccio's father was the notary Ser Giovanni di Mone (Simone) Cassai. When Ser Giovanni died in 1406, Masaccio's mother, Jacopa di Martinozzo di Dino, married Tedesco di Feo, an apothecary in Masaccio's native San Giovanni (located between Florence and Arezzo). Masaccio is recorded as a "painter" by the age of 16, and a few years later he was listed among painters in the guild records of the Arte de' Medici e Speziali. It is a tradition that Masaccio's master was Masolino, with whom he later often collaborated; however, this is unlikely because Masolino was not a member of the painters' guild until 1423, and thus would not have had apprentices during Masaccio's youth. Masaccio is often noted as the founder of Italian Renaissance painting, because he learned from the intial experiments of Giotto to create figures, drapery, and objects as fully three-dimensional with gravitational weight. In addition, his pictorial space is arranged by using linear perspective, light and shadow are portrayed as if light were entering this space from a single source, and his figures appear to be present, conscious characters who react and display emotion. Giorgio Vasari, a 16th-century biographer of artists, explained that Masaccio followed in the footsteps of sculptors Brunelleschi and Donatello, even though he worked in the medium of painting.
